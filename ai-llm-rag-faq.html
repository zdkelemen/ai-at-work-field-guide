CORE

Foundations

How the thing actually works.Q1–8
Q1

How does an LLM actually work?

A transformer predicting the next token, autoregressively. No lookup, no fact-check, no intent — optimised for fluency, not truth.

Q2

Why does next-token prediction look like reasoning?

You cannot predict text well without modelling what it is about. Reasoning was absorbed because it helps you guess well — nobody taught it directly.

Q3

Why do tokens appear on every invoice?

The token is the unit of price, latency, context and rate limits at once. Your unit economics are a token budget — and non-English text costs more per word.

Q4

What do temperature, top-p and top-k control?

How peaked the output distribution is, and how much of its tail is eligible. A consistency dial, not an accuracy dial.

Q5

What happens when you exceed the context window?

You error, or your framework silently drops the oldest messages — which is where the instructions live. Quality also degrades in the middle long before the limit.

Q6

Transformers and attention, without the maths?

Query · key · value matching, in parallel across the sequence. Cost grows quadratically with length — which explains long-context pricing, FlashAttention and the KV cache.

Q7

Pre-training vs post-training — why care?

It tells you who can fix what. Verbosity and over-refusal are post-training (tunable); missing knowledge is pre-training (needs retrieval).

Q8 · MISCONCEPTION

"The model will learn from how we use it"

It won't. Weights change only during training. Improvement needs someone to own a data flywheel — otherwise the system is as good in a year as today.

PROMPT

Prompting & structured output

What works, and what is cargo cult.Q9–14
Q9

Which prompting techniques earn their keep?

Clear task specification, few-shot examples, decomposition, schemas. Personas, emotional pressure and stacked ALL-CAPS mostly waste tokens — and stacked emphasis backfires.

Q10

Why isn't "return JSON" enough?

It works most of the time, so it breaks 0.5–5% of the time. Use constrained decoding or a strict tool schema, and put a reasoning field before the conclusions.

Q11

Does a sterner prompt fix hallucination?

No — an instruction cannot create a missing fact. What does work is an explicit "not_found" escape hatch and required quotes from supplied context.

Q12

How do you version and test prompts?

Files in git, a version ID logged on every request, an eval set in CI, staged rollout. Prompts are code, not config strings.

Q13

Why do identical prompts give different answers?

Sampling, float non-determinism, MoE routing, silent version drift, hidden context. Pin versions; never assert exact string equality.

Q14

What is grounding, and how do you measure it?

Every claim traceable to a supplied source. Measure with a fixed known-good context — and note faithful ≠ correct: a stale document grounds perfectly.

ML

ML vs GenAI

The classic toolbox, and when to reach for it.Q15–22
Q15

ML vs GenAI — the actual difference?

ML gives calibrated, auditable, near-free predictions. GenAI gives content. Classic ML for structured prediction, generative for unstructured language — use both.

Q16

Supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised, RL?

They trade labelling cost against directness of supervision. Ask what the cheapest supervision is — labels usually dominate a project's cost.

Q17

Bias–variance, and why models disappoint?

"Great in the notebook, bad in production" is almost always variance — often worsened by leakage. Split by time or entity, not randomly by row.

Q18

When do you need dimensionality reduction?

PCA to compress; UMAP/t-SNE only to look. Their axes and distances are meaningless — reading conclusions off a UMAP plot is a common, persuasive error.

Q19

How do you know a clustering is good?

Not from silhouette score. Test stability, actionability and external validation — a clustering nobody can act on is a chart, not a finding.

Q20

When is deep learning the right tool?

Unstructured high-dimensional input with real data volume. Deep learning for perception and language, boosted trees for tables, code for anything specified.

Q21

Why is transfer learning the affordability story?

It collapses data needs by orders of magnitude. Start cheap — in-context, then feature extraction, then PEFT — and test domain distance early.

Q22

What does a hybrid system look like?

LLM at the edges, ML for scoring, code for policy, humans for the tail. The demo is steps 1 and 5; the other five are the work.

RAG

RAG & retrieval

Grounding answers in real sources.Q23–31
Q23

What is RAG, and when is it right?

A search problem with a generation step attached. Most quality is in chunking, retrieval and re-ranking — not the model. Skip it if a SQL query answers the question.

Q24

What does a full pipeline look like?

Rewrite → hybrid retrieve → permission filter → re-rank → assemble → cite → validate → log. The skipped stages are always the same four, and each one costs you.

Q25

RAG, fine-tuning or prompting?

Knowledge → RAG. Behaviour → fine-tuning. Everything → prompt first. Fine-tuning to teach facts is the most expensive mistake in this space.

Q26

Why is pure vector search a demo?

It cannot find ERR_4471 — identifiers have no semantic neighbourhood. Hybrid (BM25 + vector, fused by rank) removes the most embarrassing failure class.

Q27

Why is re-ranking the biggest single win?

Retrieval scores query and document separately; a cross-encoder scores them together. Retrieve broadly for recall, re-rank narrowly for precision.

Q28

Do you need a vector database?

Often not a dedicated one. Start with what you already operate — pgvector in an existing Postgres beats adding a system until scale or hybrid quality forces it.

Q29

The two failure modes of RAG?

Retrieval failure vs generation failure. Paste the correct passage in by hand: if the answer fixes itself, it was retrieval. That two-minute test redirects weeks.

Q30

Balancing latency, relevance and cost?

Nearly every relevance lever costs the other two. Set the budget from the use case, then report quality, P95 and cost together on every change.

Q31

How do you handle constantly changing data?

Changing documents → event-driven reindex with validity timestamps. Live state → don't index it; call the system of record via a tool.

CONTEXT

Context & memory

Deciding what the model can see.Q32–35
Q32

Context engineering vs prompt engineering?

Prompting crafts the instruction; context engineering is the runtime policy deciding what the model sees on every call, under a hard budget. It's architecture, not text.

Q33

What goes in, and in what priority?

Policy → tools → task state → evidence → recent turns → summary → memory → few-shot. Keep the prefix byte-identical or caching silently dies.

Q34

How do you give a system memory?

Five layers; the hard part is policy — what to write, when to recall, how to resolve contradictions, how users delete it. Bad memory is worse than none.

Q35

Long context or RAG?

Long context wins for bounded, shared, slow-changing corpora. Per-user permissions rule it out — you cannot cache a shared prefix of documents users may not all see.

AGENTS

Agents, tools & protocols

Where AI touches the real world.Q36–42
Q36

What happens when a feature "calls a tool"?

Eight steps, and most people picture three. Validation, authorisation and tracing are where incidents are prevented — and they're invisible in a demo.

Q37

Does the model execute the function?

No — it emits an intent; your code validates, authorises and executes. "The AI issued a wrong refund" is a missing check, never a model failure.

Q38

Why is a vague tool description a bug?

It's runtime input, not a comment. Say when NOT to use each tool — distinguishing similar tools is often the highest-leverage ten minutes in an agent.

Q39

Agent, workflow or chatbot?

Errors compound (0.95⁵ ≈ 0.77) and cost is unbounded. Ask "what's the least agency that solves this?" — usually a workflow with one agentic step.

Q40

Prompt vs tool vs skill?

Prompt = how it behaves. Tool = what it can do, and the trust boundary. Skill = how we do this task here, loaded on demand and reviewable in a PR.

Q41

MCP, A2A — does the protocol layer matter yet?

MCP has real adoption and turns N×M into N+M. Agent-to-agent standards are genuinely early — designing around them in 2026 is research, not engineering.

Q42

How do you coordinate multiple agents?

One orchestrator owns state; each sub-agent gets a narrow scope and a structured schema — never prose hand-off, which is where these systems rot.

ORCH

Orchestration & gateways

Frameworks, graphs and the proxy layer.Q43–47
Q43

Which agent framework should you use?

LangGraph for stateful graphs, CrewAI for role fan-out, Microsoft Agent Framework in .NET estates, LlamaIndex for RAG-heavy, Pydantic AI for typed Python, Mastra/Vercel for TypeScript. If it hides your prompts, wrong layer.

Q44

Chain or state graph?

Chains for pipelines, graphs for state machines. And consider a durable workflow engine (Temporal, Dagster, Prefect) — long-running with human waits is their problem, not AI's.

Q45

When is orchestration over-engineering?

When one prompt would work better. Point at each node's measured contribution — if a node exists because the diagram looked tidier, delete it.

Q46

Do you need an LLM gateway?

LiteLLM self-hosted, OpenRouter hosted aggregation, Portkey for guardrails, Cloudflare at the edge, or your existing API gateway. Not for one app and one provider.

Q47

The four layers — where do you invest?

Compute → model → orchestration → experience. You spend on 1–2 and differentiate on 3–4, so stay portable below and invest above.

DATA

Data & pipelines

What your AI feature actually stands on.Q48–54
Q48

ETL or ELT — why did it switch?

Cheap decoupled storage made "load raw, transform in SQL" viable. Raw data preserved means you can re-derive everything — at the cost of active retention discipline.

Q49

What does dbt actually solve?

Not running SQL. It gave analytics code version control, tests, dependency management, docs and lineage. Alternatives: SQLMesh, Dataform, Coalesce, Dagster assets.

Q50

How do you get data in?

Fivetran (buy maintenance), Airbyte (open, self-hostable), dlt (pipelines as code), Meltano, CDC via Debezium. Monitor freshness and row counts — ingestion fails silently.

Q51

Warehouse, lake or lakehouse?

For AI work what matters is Python/Spark readability, point-in-time queries and repeated-scan cost. Iceberg-style open formats keep the most options open.

Q52

Do you need a feature store?

It kills training/serving skew — the same feature implemented twice, slightly differently. Feast, Tecton, Hopsworks, or platform-native. Not for your first model.

Q53 · MISCONCEPTION

"The model broke"

Usually it didn't. The overwhelming majority of "model" incidents are data incidents — a column arriving null raises no exception, it just degrades predictions.

Q54

Pipeline that feeds a retrieval index?

Ingest → parse → metadata incl. access control → chunk with stable IDs → embed deltas → upsert and propagate deletes → validate recall. And keep a full-rebuild path.

TUNING

Fine-tuning

When it's worth it, and what it costs.Q55–59
Q55

When is fine-tuning actually right?

Rigid format, narrow high-volume task, or moving a long prompt into weights. A fine-tune has a shelf life — the next general model may beat it for free.

Q56

LoRA, QLoRA, PEFT — what changed?

Train a tiny added set of parameters over a frozen (often 4-bit) base. Adapters are small files, so many task-specific tunes can share one base model.

Q57

Which fine-tuning tools?

Unsloth (fastest, 1–2 GPU), Axolotl (multi-GPU production), TRL (objective control), LLaMA-Factory (breadth), torchtune, or managed. Budget as much time for the eval set as for training.

Q58

What data do you need, and from where?

Hundreds to low thousands — but consistency matters more than volume. Measure inter-annotator agreement first: it is the ceiling on achievable quality.

Q59

RLHF, DPO, GRPO — do you care?

Mostly not, unless you build models. But if you log thumbs plus the rejected alternative, you have preference data — most organisations throw that asset away.

SERVING

Inference, serving & local models

Running it yourself, or not.Q60–65
Q60

When should you run models locally?

Data can't leave, offline needed, or high volume and low complexity. At low utilisation an API is almost always cheaper — idle GPUs cost the same as busy ones.

Q61

Ollama, LM Studio, Jan, llama.cpp?

Different layers, not competitors: llama.cpp is the engine, the others are experience layers over it (plus LocalAI, llamafile, MLX on Apple). None is a multi-user serving system.

Q62

How do you serve many users at once?

vLLM by default, SGLang for prefix-heavy RAG, TensorRT-LLM on NVIDIA, TGI, LMDeploy — or managed inference. Measure throughput at your real concurrency, not single-request latency.

Q63

What does quantisation cost you?

Degradation is task-dependent: chat barely changes while instruction-following, structured output and long-context recall degrade first — exactly what production needs.

Q64

Hosted or self-hosted — the arithmetic?

API cost scales with usage; self-hosting is a capacity bet where utilisation is the metric. Batch workloads self-host well; spiky interactive ones badly.

Q65

What about small models and the edge?

Strong on narrow high-volume tasks after fine-tuning; weak beyond their range, where they produce confident nonsense rather than graceful degradation.

EVAL

Evaluation

How you know it's any good.Q66–72
Q66

How do you evaluate before launch?

Golden set → automatic metrics → human review → adversarial pass. Set the launch bar before you look at results, or every result becomes acceptable.

Q67

How do you build the framework?

A pyramid, plus an error taxonomy and per-slice reporting — averages hide the failures that generate complaints. 92% overall can be 55% in one language.

Q68

LLM-as-judge — when can you trust it?

Checkable criteria, measured human agreement, pairwise not 1–10, different model. A smoke alarm, not a courtroom. Control for position, verbosity and self-preference bias.

Q69

Precision, recall, F1 — plainly?

F1 quietly assumes both matter equally, which is almost never true. Ask which error costs more; then the threshold is arithmetic — and it's a business decision.

Q70

Evaluating something non-deterministic?

Assert properties, run n samples, report variance as a metric. Declaring a 2% win on 50 examples is the classic error — it's inside noise.

Q71

Which eval and observability tools?

Langfuse (self-host), LangSmith (LangChain stacks), Braintrust (CI-gated), Phoenix (OTel-native), Weave, MLflow; or libraries — Ragas, DeepEval, promptfoo. Start with tracing.

Q72

Evals pass but users are unhappy?

Read 50 real transcripts before touching anything. Usual cause: the eval set doesn't match traffic, or you measured the wrong metric.

MLOPS

Observability & MLOps

Keeping it alive after launch.Q73–78
Q73

MLOps vs DevOps, and what does LLMOps add?

Three things change instead of one, quality is a threshold not a tick, and ML systems degrade with nobody touching them. LLMOps adds prompt versioning and vendor dependency.

Q74

What do you version?

Code, data snapshot, model (pinned — never a floating -latest), prompts, retrieval config, eval sets. Test: could you reproduce a request from three weeks ago?

Q75

What is drift, and how do you see it?

Data, concept and prediction drift — plus prompt drift and silent vendor model drift. Alert on rate of change, or the system gets muted.

Q76

What must monitoring cover?

Infra, cost, quality proxies and outcome, over full traces. A wrong answer throws no exception — and sample errors at 100%, normal traffic low.

Q77

How do you ship a change safely?

Golden set side by side per slice → shadow → 5% canary → ramp. And prompts are model-specific: same prompt plus new model is a different system.

Q78

How do you handle an AI incident?

The system is up — answering fast and wrong. Mitigate before you explain, but capture failing traces first, or you lose the evidence.

COST

Cost & FinOps

What makes an AI feature affordable.Q79–83
Q79

How do you estimate the bill?

MAU × calls × tokens × price, adjusted for retries, caching and routing — then sanity-check against revenue per user. That last step is the finding.

Q80

Why does routing decide affordability?

70–90% of real traffic is easy. Route it cheap and cost drops several-fold. Bias the router toward escalation, and monitor quality per route — decay hides in the cheap path.

Q81

The real levers on cost and latency?

Prompt caching (keep the prefix byte-identical), routing, semantic caching, token discipline, batch APIs, streaming. The strongest is deleting the wait entirely.

Q82

How do you keep spend under control?

Visibility, hard budgets at the gateway, anomaly alerts, unit economics. Per-request ceilings on tokens, tool calls and steps — agentic loops are how budgets vanish.

Q83

Models improve constantly. What holds value?

The workflow, the data flywheel, the eval harness and domain knowledge. Abstract the model — then an upgrade is a half-day dividend, not a project.

AUTO

Automation tooling

n8n, Zapier, Make, Windmill, Airflow, Temporal…Q84–89
Q84

What is n8n, and the honest alternatives?

Hosted: Zapier (widest catalogue), Make, Power Automate, Workato. Open/self-host: n8n, Activepieces, Windmill (code-first), Pipedream, Kestra, Node-RED. AI-native: Flowise, Langflow, Dify.

Q85

How do you choose a platform?

Who maintains it, where data must live, how critical, how complex. Prototype visually, then rewrite the two business-critical flows as tested code.

Q86

What should you automate first?

Frequent, judgement-light, tolerant-failure tasks with a clear trigger. Automating a broken process just makes it fail faster — and harder to inspect.

Q87

How do you make it reliable?

Idempotency keys on every write, capped retries, explicit failure paths, validation between steps, loop and cost limits — plus a named owner and a review date.

Q88

Where do agents fit versus fixed flows?

Deterministic flow, model-powered steps. Unattended agency is worse than interactive: nobody is watching to notice the strange path.

Q89

Is RPA obsolete?

No, but narrowed. API → database → file exchange → UI automation, in that order. Vision models make UI driving less brittle and the alternatives more attractive.

MODELS

Model & tool landscape

Mid-2026 snapshot — verify before committing.Q90–94
Q90

The main model families?

Claude, GPT, Gemini, Grok, Llama, Mistral, Cohere — each with an ecosystem centre of gravity. Tiers within a family differ more than families differ.

Q91

Chinese open-weight models — why do they matter?

DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, GLM, MiniMax, MiMo. Cost pressure and self-hosting. Open weights ≠ open data or permissive licence — and hosted API ≠ self-hosted weights.

Q92

Which AI coding tools, and how do they differ?

Copilot, Cursor, Windsurf, Zed, Cody, Continue; CLIs: Claude Code, Codex, Antigravity, Aider, Cline, OpenHands. Axes: where it runs, model flexibility, permissions, repo-scale context.

Q93

What happened to the Gemini CLI?

Retired for consumers on 18 June 2026, replaced by the Go-based Antigravity CLI. Scripts on the old one need a real migration — keep CI integrations thin and pinned.

Q94

How do you choose a model?

Eval set first, probe the ceiling, optimise down, route, keep an exit. Public benchmarks do not transfer — and test in the language you actually ship in.

SECURITY

Security & safety

The failures that actually cost money.Q95–98
Q95

What are the real risks?

Leakage, wrong output acted on, injection, excessive permission, shadow AI, compliance. Ask per feature: what data does it see, and what can it change?

Q96

How do you defend against prompt injection?

Least privilege, confirmation for consequential actions, server-side policy, egress restriction. Treat any text your agent reads as untrusted user input.

Q97

What guardrails and supply-chain controls?

Deterministic validation first, classifiers where risk justifies latency — and measure the false-positive rate. Model weights and MCP servers are third-party code.

Q98

Where does a human stay in the loop?

Reversibility × cost of error. Measure reviewer disagreement — if it trends to zero you have rubber-stamping, which manufactures false confidence.

GOV

Governance, legal & organisation

Policy, regulation, adoption.Q99–100
Q99

AI policy, and what does the EU AI Act require?

Risk-tiered obligations; high-risk duties were slated for August 2026 but the timeline was under active revision — verify current status rather than planning on a date. Start by inventorying your AI systems and determining your role per system.

Q100

How do you roll this out without it flopping?

Named pain, one workflow deep, baseline measured first, the doers involved, honest about failure modes — and teach the mental model, not the buttons.

UX

Product & UX patterns

Whether a technically good feature gets used.appendix
UX-1

Show progress, not a spinner

Streaming doesn't reduce real latency but transforms perceived latency. Where you can't stream, show what the system is doing — visible work buys patience.

UX-2

Make uncertainty specific

"I couldn't find this in the policy documents" beats a hedged answer. Blanket disclaimers on every response train users to ignore them.

UX-3

Put evidence next to claims

Inline citations beside the statement, not collected at the bottom. The most effective trust affordance — and it makes review faster, so review actually happens.

UX-4

Design for correction, not just output

Editable output, one-click regenerate, undo. Capture the edit diff — it is your best quality signal. Never discard a user's work because the model was wrong.

UX-5

Capture feedback implicitly

Accepted, edited, regenerated, abandoned, copied — free, unbiased, on every interaction. Explicit thumbs are sparse and biased toward extremes.

Costly beliefs

Five beliefs that cause real damage

  1. "The model will learn from our usage." Weights change only during training. Without a data flywheel, nothing improves by itself.
  2. "We'll fine-tune on our data so it knows our business." Fine-tuning teaches behaviour, not facts. Knowledge is retrieval.
  3. "We'll instruct it not to hallucinate." An instruction cannot create a missing fact. Give it an explicit not-found path instead.
  4. "The model broke." Most "model" incidents are data incidents. Check the upstream pipeline before retraining anything.
  5. "Thumbs-up/down will tell us if it's working." That signal is sparse and biased. You need a golden set, a launch bar and a regression gate.
